# Prior Research on Advice and Claimed Source Reliability

Targeted primary-source comparison prepared on 2026-10-19. The project is a replication and extension of existing research on advice taking, source authority, and explanation faithfulness. Neither sensitivity to stated reliability nor susceptibility to incorrect hints is a first-discovery claim. This search is targeted rather than a systematic literature review.

## Defensible contribution

The completed experiments document how three specific model deployments respond to answer-shaped metadata and numerical credibility claims on two-choice cybersecurity recall questions. The clearer wording, tag check, direct model comparison, and follow-up with two new paraphrases support a specific replication and extension. The held-out paraphrases are new wordings on previously used questions; they do not establish transfer to a new domain.

The paused extension adds unused project questions and correct-key conditions, but its 623 saved responses do not yet support the planned balanced net-accuracy endpoint. The recent medical factorial study means that even completing the extension would not justify claiming the first correctness-by-reliability experiment.

An appropriate central claim is: “In these model deployments and question settings, answer-key presentation and claimed reliability influence answer selection, with different patterns across models and wordings.” Stronger claims about rational trust, hidden cognition, instruction-hierarchy failure, or model-wide safety require evidence beyond these experiments.

## Differences that matter for interpretation

The project manipulates a textual claim about a source. The paused study independently balances actual key correctness, so a 95% label is not an empirically verified 95%-accurate source. Separate control repeats are noisy behavioral measurements, not calibrated model confidence. A fitted cue coefficient near a Bayesian-looking value therefore does not by itself establish rational integration.

Task-interpretation labels describe returned explanations. They cannot distinguish internal obedience from post hoc justification. A new intervention would need to vary the instruction about the key while holding its content, stated reliability, and question fixed. Such a study would remain connected to existing instruction-hierarchy and advice-taking research.

Before an external novelty claim, expand the search systematically, retrieve the medical factorial paper's full methods, and check additional work under credibility, persuasion, compliance, authority bias, and knowledge conflict. These are unresolved literature-review tasks, not reasons to purchase more model responses now.
